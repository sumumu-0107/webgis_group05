PM₁₀ Results
1. PM₁₀ Concentration in December 2023

The December 2023 PM₁₀ concentration map shows that PM₁₀ levels were unevenly distributed across Greece. Higher concentration values appear mainly in northern and central Greece, where a combination of urban heating emissions, road traffic, agricultural activities, and dust resuspension contribute to elevated levels during winter months. Thessaloniki and the surrounding Thessaly plain show notably higher PM₁₀ values, reflecting both industrial activity and seasonal heating. Coastal and island areas tend to exhibit lower PM₁₀ concentrations, benefiting from sea breeze dispersion. The map highlights that PM₁₀ pollution in winter is not limited to large cities but also affects agricultural lowlands and valleys where meteorological conditions trap particulate matter near the surface.

2. Annual Average PM₁₀ Concentration in 2023

The annual average PM₁₀ map shows the general spatial pattern of PM₁₀ concentration across Greece in 2023. The highest average values are concentrated in northern Greece, particularly around Thessaloniki, the Thessaly plain, and parts of Central Macedonia, where a combination of industrial emissions, traffic, construction activity, and agricultural burning contribute to sustained particulate matter levels throughout the year. The Greater Athens area also shows elevated annual average values, though the dispersion effect of coastal winds partially reduces peak concentrations. Southern Greece and the Aegean islands generally show lower annual averages, though occasional Saharan dust intrusion events cause temporary increases across the entire country. The annual average map provides a more representative picture of chronic exposure compared to any single monthly snapshot.

3. PM₁₀ Concentration Classes in 2023

The PM₁₀ concentration class map divides the annual average concentration into air quality classes. In Greece, the visible PM₁₀ classes are mainly Class 1, Class 2, and Class 3, while Class 4 and Class 5 are absent or extremely limited in spatial extent. Class 1 covers large portions of mountainous regions, island territories, and southern coastal areas where PM₁₀ levels remain low throughout the year. Class 2 dominates many mid-sized urban and suburban zones, as well as agricultural lowlands in central and northern Greece. Class 3 appears primarily in the most urbanized and industrialized parts of the country, especially around Thessaloniki and Greater Athens. The classification reveals that the majority of the Greek territory maintains relatively moderate PM₁₀ levels on an annual basis, though localized hotspots in dense urban environments remain a concern for air quality management.

4. PM₁₀ Annual Mean Aggregation Change, 2021–2023

The PM₁₀ Annual Mean Aggregation Change map shows both increases and decreases in PM₁₀ concentration between 2021 and 2023. Some areas in northern and central Greece show increases in PM₁₀, particularly around zones that experienced expanded construction activity, new road infrastructure, or increased agricultural land disturbance. In contrast, parts of Attica and several island areas show slight decreases, possibly reflecting improved vehicle emission standards, reduced construction activity, or favorable meteorological conditions in 2023 compared to 2021. The change pattern is spatially heterogeneous, indicating that PM₁₀ trends are strongly influenced by local activity and land use changes rather than a uniform national trend. Overall, the magnitude of change is moderate, with most grid cells showing relatively small absolute differences between the two years.

5. Built Area Change in Greece, 2021–2023

The built area change map identifies Stable Built Area, Built Area Gain, and Built Area Loss between 2021 and 2023. Most built-up pixels are classified as Stable Built Area, showing that existing urban footprints remained largely unchanged during this period. Built Area Gain pixels are scattered across suburban fringes of major cities and along transportation corridors, reflecting ongoing urban expansion and new construction projects. Built Area Loss pixels are relatively few and are often associated with demolition, land remediation, or reclassification of previously built land. The built area change analysis provides the spatial framework for understanding how PM₁₀ emission sources related to construction and urban dust may have shifted between the two reference years.

6. PM₁₀ Change Statistics by Built Area Transition Type

The bar chart compares the mean, minimum, and maximum PM₁₀ change values for Stable Built Area, Built Area Gain, and Built Area Loss. Built Area Gain has the highest mean PM₁₀ change among the three transition types, reflecting that areas undergoing new construction tend to experience increased particulate matter from ground disturbance, material handling, and construction machinery. Stable Built Area shows a moderate mean PM₁₀ change, consistent with gradual shifts in traffic volume and heating emissions. Built Area Loss shows the smallest or slightly negative mean PM₁₀ change, suggesting that areas where built surfaces were removed may have seen reduced dust generation from hard surfaces. The pattern confirms that land cover transitions, particularly urban expansion, are associated with detectable changes in local PM₁₀ levels.

7. PM₁₀–Population Bivariate Map

The PM₁₀–Population bivariate map combines PM₁₀ concentration class and population class at the administrative zone level. The first digit of each bivariate value represents the PM₁₀ class, while the second digit represents the population class, both ranging from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest). Administrative zones with high bivariate values in both dimensions indicate areas of greatest public health concern, where high PM₁₀ concentrations coincide with large population numbers. These zones are found predominantly around Thessaloniki and metropolitan Athens. Zones along the northern Greek periphery show elevated PM₁₀ classes but relatively smaller population classes, suggesting that agricultural and semi-urban populations are exposed to moderate particulate pollution. Rural and island zones typically show low values in both dimensions, indicating both lower pollution and lower population density.

8. Bivariate Legend Interpretation

The 5×5 bivariate legend explains how the two variables are combined in the bivariate exposure map. The vertical axis represents pollutant concentration, increasing from low (Class 1) to high (Class 5) from bottom to top, while the horizontal axis represents population class, increasing from low to high from left to right. Each cell in the legend corresponds to a unique combination of pollution and population class. Cells in the lower-left corner represent low PM₁₀ and low population zones, while cells in the upper-right corner represent the highest concern areas combining elevated PM₁₀ with dense population. The colour scheme uses a diverging two-axis gradient, allowing map readers to quickly identify which zones require priority attention for air quality interventions.

9. Population Exposure to PM₁₀ Concentration Classes

The PM₁₀ population exposure pie chart shows how the population is distributed across the PM₁₀ concentration classes. The results indicate that a large share of the population resides in Class 1 or Class 2 zones, meaning the majority of Greeks are exposed to relatively moderate annual PM₁₀ levels. A meaningful portion of the population, primarily concentrated in major urban agglomerations, falls within Class 3, where PM₁₀ levels are elevated enough to warrant monitoring and mitigation measures. Very few or no residents are found in Class 4 or Class 5, as these extreme concentration classes are not observed at the annual average scale in Greece. The exposure summary confirms that while PM₁₀ pollution is present and spatially variable, severe chronic exposure affects only a minority of the population, mainly in the most industrialized and densely built urban areas.

10. Overall PM₁₀ Interpretation

Overall, the PM₁₀ results show a clear relationship between land use patterns, construction activity, population distribution, and particulate matter exposure in Greece. The concentration maps show that higher PM₁₀ values are spatially associated with northern lowland regions, major urban agglomerations, and areas undergoing active land development, rather than being strictly limited to city centres as seen with traffic-related pollutants like NO₂. The annual change analysis between 2021 and 2023 indicates that PM₁₀ trends are driven by a combination of urban expansion, agricultural emissions, and interannual variability in dust events. The bivariate exposure mapping reveals that the population clusters most exposed to elevated PM₁₀ are located in Greater Thessaloniki and the Athens basin, where high pollutant levels and large residential populations overlap. Rural and island populations generally experience lower PM₁₀ exposure. These findings highlight the importance of integrated land use management, construction dust regulation, and emissions control in Greece's densely populated northern urban zones.

11. Short Summary for Website Card

The PM₁₀ analysis shows that higher concentrations are mainly found in northern Greece and major urban areas, while island and mountainous regions remain in lower concentration classes. Between 2021 and 2023, areas experiencing new construction and urban expansion recorded the most notable increases in PM₁₀ levels. The bivariate mapping reveals that Thessaloniki and Athens face the greatest combined burden of high particulate matter and large exposed populations. Most of the Greek population is exposed to Class 1 or Class 2 PM₁₀ levels, though urban residents in the north face elevated chronic exposure that warrants targeted air quality measures.
