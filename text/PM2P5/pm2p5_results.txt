PM2.5 and Crops Results

1. PM2.5 Concentration in December 2023
The December 2023 PM2.5 concentration map shows that PM2.5 levels were unevenly distributed across Greece. Higher concentration values appear mainly around major urban and transport-related areas, especially around the Athens metropolitan area and parts of northern Greece. In contrast, many island and rural regions show lower PM2.5 values. This pattern suggests that short-term PM2.5 concentration is strongly influenced by urban activity, traffic intensity, and regional emission sources. The December map provides a monthly snapshot before the annual average concentration was calculated.

2. Annual Average PM2.5 Concentration in 2023
The annual average PM2.5 map shows the general spatial pattern of PM2.5 concentration across Greece in 2023. The highest average values are concentrated around large urban areas, especially the Athens region, while lower values are found in less urbanized mainland areas and many islands. Compared with the monthly December map, the annual average map smooths short-term fluctuations and highlights more stable spatial patterns. This makes it more suitable for classification and population exposure analysis. The range of values also shows that PM2.5 concentration varies clearly between urbanized and non-urbanized areas.

3. PM2.5 Concentration Classes in 2023
The PM2.5 concentration class map divides the annual average concentration into air quality classes. In Greece, the visible PM2.5 classes are mainly Class 1, Class 2, and Class 3, while Class 4 and Class 5 do not appear in the mapped result. Most of the country falls into Class 2 and Class 3, indicating intermediate PM2.5 concentration levels between 5 µg/m³ and 20 µg/m³. Class 1 areas are more limited and are mainly located around specific island regions or high-altitude ecosystems. This classification makes the spatial pattern easier to interpret and provides the pollutant class input for the bivariate population exposure map.

4. PM2.5 Annual Mean Aggregation Change, 2021–2023
The PM2.5 Annual Mean Aggregation Change map shows both increases and decreases in PM2.5 concentration between 2021 and 2023. Some areas in western Greece show significant increases, forming a distinct hotspot, where atmospheric conditions have worsened since 2021. Conversely, the eastern administrative regions and coastal plains show neutral to stabilizing trends (0 to -1.5 µg/m³), mapping localized air quality stabilization over the three-year timeline. Stronger increases are visible in localized zones, suggesting that PM2.5 change was not uniform across the country. This map is important because it shows not only where PM2.5 was high in 2023, but also where the situation changed compared with 2021.

5. Crop Area Change in Greece, 2021–2023
The crop area change map identifies Stable Crop Area, Crop Area Gain, and Crop Area Loss between 2021 and 2023. Most cultivated pixels are classified as Stable Crop Area, showing that existing agricultural plains in Thessaly and Central Macedonia remained largely unchanged during the study period. Crop Area Gain and Crop Area Loss appear as scattered and localized changes rather than large continuous zones. These transition classes are useful because agricultural emissions, vegetative feedback, and field management practices are often related to land dynamics. Therefore, the crop area change layer was used as the basis for comparing PM2.5 change statistics across different agricultural transition types.

6. PM2.5 Change Statistics by Crop Area Transition Type
The bar chart compares the mean, minimum, and maximum PM2.5 change values for Stable Crop Area, Crop Area Gain, and Crop Area Loss. Stable Crop Area has a mean PM2.5 change of approximately 0.226 µg/m³, followed by Crop Area Gain at about 0.088 µg/m³, while Crop Area Loss displays a negative mean divergence of -0.101 µg/m³. This means that, on average, PM2.5 experienced the least growth in areas of newly gained crop cover, showing a relative buffering signature. However, all three transition classes share identical local variance bounds, with the minimum value consistently dipping to -4.392 µg/m³ and the maximum value reaching +4.717 µg/m³ across the zones.

7. PM2.5–Population Bivariate Map
The PM2.5–Population bivariate map combines PM2.5 concentration class and population class at the administrative zone level. The first digit of each bivariate value represents the PM2.5 class, while the second digit represents the population class. In the Greece PM2.5 result, the visible combinations include 33, 34, and 35, meaning that several administrative regions have a baseline level of Class 3 pollution but differ widely in their demographic weights. The class 35 areas are especially important because they combine a higher PM2.5 class with a high population class, capturing critical urban centers like Athens. This map helps identify where air pollution and population exposure overlap, rather than only showing pollution concentration alone.

8. Bivariate Legend Interpretation
The 5×5 bivariate legend explains how the two variables are combined in the bivariate exposure map. The vertical axis represents pollutant concentration, increasing from low to high, while the horizontal axis represents population count, also increasing from low to high. Colors toward the upper-right part of the legend indicate areas where both pollutant concentration and population count are relatively high. In the PM2.5 map, this legend helps interpret why some regions require more attention even if they are not the largest regions spatially. The legend is essential because the bivariate map cannot be correctly understood from color alone.

9. Population Exposure to PM2.5 Concentration Classes
The PM2.5 population exposure chart shows how the population is distributed across the PM2.5 concentration classes. The results indicate that exactly 100% of the calculated population is exposed to Class 3 (>10 and <=20 µg/m³). This means that the entire shared population falls in Class 3 due to the uniform spatial distribution of this regulatory tier over populated provincial zones. The result suggests that a considerable part of the population is exposed to higher PM2.5 concentration classes, even though much of the territory is mapped as low concentration. This difference highlights why population-weighted exposure analysis is necessary in addition to simple spatial mapping.

10. Overall PM2.5 Interpretation
Overall, the PM2.5 results show a clear relationship between urbanization, population distribution, and air pollution exposure in Greece. The concentration maps show that higher PM2.5 values are spatially concentrated around urban areas, while the bivariate map shows where these pollution levels overlap with population density. The crop area transition analysis suggests that PM2.5 changes are not only related to newly developed areas, but also to stable and changing rural zones. The population exposure result further shows that spatial area and exposed population are not the same: a small high-concentration area may still affect a large number of people. Therefore, combining pollutant maps, land cover change, zonal statistics, and population exposure provides a more complete understanding of PM2.5 conditions in Greece.

11. Short Summary for Website Card
The PM2.5 analysis shows that higher concentrations are mainly concentrated around urbanized regions, while many rural and island areas remain in lower concentration classes. Between 2021 and 2023, PM2.5 changes were spatially uneven, with both increases and decreases across different parts of Greece. Crop Area Gain showed a suppressed PM2.5 increase compared with Stable Crop Area, highlighting localized vegetative feedback. The population exposure analysis indicates that 100% of the population was exposed to PM2.5 Class 3. These results highlight the importance of combining air quality data with land cover transition and population exposure analysis.
